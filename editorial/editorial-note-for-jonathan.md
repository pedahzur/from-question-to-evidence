# Note for Jonathan

Dear Jonathan,

I recently returned to the materials we assembled for the research-methods book about a decade ago. The archive was more substantial than I remembered. It contains long drafts on literature review and research preparation, shorter sections on field collection and post-production, cases drawn from terrorism data, and a large catalogue of software. It also contains duplicates, abandoned sections, copied articles, and product descriptions that now read as a history of the late 2010s.

The attached discussion draft does not restore that manuscript page by page. It recovers what I think was our strongest idea: researchers need a sequence for moving from an expansive question to a body of evidence whose composition and limits they can explain. The new organizing artifact is an evidence map. It links the question to concepts, source families, search decisions, evaluated records, gaps, and a stopping rule.

Generative AI enters as a research aid rather than the book's subject. The draft offers bounded uses for question decomposition, vocabulary expansion, missing-perspective checks, and gap analysis. It insists on verification, privacy, and an audit trail. This approach should remain useful when current products disappear.

I used our terrorism-data material as the running example because it shows how definitions, originating sources, collection systems, and coding rules produce different accounts of the same phenomenon. I avoided reproducing third-party PDFs, screenshots, and copied web material from the archive. The current draft uses only rewritten prose and verified source metadata.

The pilot is designed for two audiences. Early-career researchers receive an explicit path and a concrete artifact at each stage. Experienced researchers receive shorter methodological disputes, team protocols, and difficult cases. Both follow the same seven-stage movement.

I would value your judgment on five questions. First, does the evidence map recover the central contribution we wanted the original book to make? Second, should we retain the terrorism-data example, replace it, or pair it with a case farther from our own research? Third, does the draft give experienced researchers enough intellectual value while remaining usable for graduate students? Fourth, how should we describe authorship and editorial responsibility if we continue the project together? Fifth, which later module would you most want to develop: literature review, field collection, preparing unstructured material, analysis, or communication and preservation?

The accompanying website design treats the book as an open-access living field guide. The methodological chapters would change through reviewed releases. Templates and tool cards could be updated more often. English would come first, with Hebrew later. No public launch is implied by this package. It is meant to give us something concrete to discuss.

Warmly,

Ami
