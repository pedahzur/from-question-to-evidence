# Source Review: Big Data, Networked Source Management, and Historical Evidence

## Materials reviewed

1. Jonathan Grossman and Ami Pedahzur, “Political Science and Big Data:
   Structured Data, Unstructured Data, and How to Use Them,” *Political Science
   Quarterly* 135, no. 2 (2020): 225–257.
2. Jonathan Grossman, Orel B. Amano, and Ami Pedahzur, “Managing High-Volume
   Digital Sources in Political Research with Emerging Technologies,” working
   paper, January 2022, SSRN 4012684.
3. *Historical Evidence Chain: Salem Witch Trials Pilot*, repository version
   0.1.0 at commit `488aa604ba8f0ec4fb76232f15d3f15835749c62`.

The PDFs remain in the private Methods Textbook project. They are cited but not
redistributed through the public repository.

## Argument extracted from the 2020 article

The article rejects two shortcuts. Big data do not remove the need for theory,
context, source criticism, or within-case analysis. At the same time, historically
oriented social scientists cannot ignore large unstructured collections merely
because those collections resist conventional tabular analysis.

Its strongest book-level contributions are:

- the distinction between structured data and unstructured sources;
- the claim that structuring is reductive and cannot be reversed without the
  original material;
- the warning that large datasets inherit reporting, access, selection, coding,
  and dependence problems;
- the combination of large-scale retrieval with close reading;
- the value of unstructured big data for historical institutionalism and process
  tracing;
- the need for detailed timelines, rival hypotheses, context, and independent
  corroboration; and
- the proposal for qualitative codebooks and linked source locators that retain a
  route back to the original source.

The article's internal progression is useful for the book. It defines the object,
tests the dominant enthusiasm, shows where structured data fail, introduces
concrete historical cases, and ends with a practical method whose limits remain
visible.

## Argument extracted from the 2022 working paper

The working paper moves from analysis to the pre-analytical phase. It asks how a
researcher can gather, organize, retrieve, compare, and preserve large volumes of
digital primary and secondary sources before formal analysis begins.

Its strongest contributions are:

- the distinction between born-digital and digitized sources;
- digital searchability as both an opportunity and a source of confirmation bias;
- the paradox of plenty, in which more searchable material makes systematic
  comparison harder;
- expertise, language, context, and close reading as non-delegable capacities;
- networked PKM as a way to connect source excerpts, entities, annotations,
  databases, and prior projects;
- the separation of reference management, archiving, and PKM functions;
- the warning against indiscriminate capture and live-source dependence; and
- the long-term value of a research memory that can reconnect old sources with new
  questions.

This paper supplies the connective tissue between the evidence map and later AI
workflows. The PKM is not an answer engine. It is the persistent substrate on which
bounded retrieval, transformation, comparison, and audit can operate.

## Contribution of the Historical Evidence Chain project

The historical project supplies the implementation that the earlier papers could
not yet provide. It separates source witness, diplomatic transcription, passage,
observation, normalization, inference, validation, historical claim, and audit.
It adds stable identifiers, append-only correction, document-specific roles,
parallel human coding, versioned prompts, relational schemas, controlled
vocabularies, uncertainty, and downstream dependency checking.

Its present limits must remain explicit. Salem is a proposed test setting, not an
empirical finding. The repository contains no source images, model outputs,
adjudicated reference set, or completed evaluation. Its value to the textbook is
methodological and pedagogical.

## Writing-style findings

The papers share an identifiable approach, although coauthored prose cannot be
assigned to one author. Both begin with a practical research pressure, define a
contested term before using it analytically, and move through contrasts rather than
announcements. Structured and unstructured data, abundance and overload,
searchability and context, automation and judgment, hierarchy and network each
form a productive pair.

Concrete cases carry the abstract argument. The prose moves from a method claim to
a political or historical example and then returns to the consequence for research
design. Technical concepts enter through ordinary language. Warnings follow
promises closely, which prevents technological enthusiasm from becoming the
argument. The preferred authorial position is collective and direct: “we argue,”
“we propose,” and “we do not recommend.”

The textbook should preserve those strengths while tightening features of the
earlier journal register. Long roadmaps, literature lists in the body, repeated
adverbs, and sentences carrying several evidentiary claims should be reduced.
Names of scholars should move to notes when the identity is not part of the
argument. Each chapter should state its mechanism early, use fewer developed
examples, and end with an artifact and decision rule.

## Editorial consequences

The merged book should not become a digital-humanities survey or software manual.
Its distinctive object is the complete historically grounded research cycle under
conditions of source abundance and probabilistic assistance. The stable book
teaches decisions and evidence relations. Versioned labs teach implementation.

The two papers should become foundational citations in the opening, historical
sources chapter, and PKM chapter. The 2020 paper carries the structured and
unstructured big-data argument. The 2022 paper carries the networked source
management argument. The Historical Evidence Chain lab carries operational
instruments and remains explicitly provisional until a pilot is run.
