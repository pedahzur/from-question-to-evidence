# When the Archive Does Not Use Our Words

While researching the Field Companies and the Special Night Squads, I encountered a familiar problem. The story preserved in the Hebrew press did not appear in the Arabic press under the same institutional names. Searches for Arabic equivalents of “Special Night Squads” produced almost nothing useful. Possible translations of “Field Companies” did no better. It would have been easy to read this as an absence of coverage. More likely, it was a failure of the researcher’s vocabulary.

I therefore stopped searching for the organizations and searched for the events in which they operated: Hanita, the oil pipeline, Kawkab al-Hawa, Dabburiya, night patrols, ambushes, searches, arrests, and casualties. This shift revealed a small but consequential set of Arabic reports from 1938. The Hebrew retrospective emphasized leadership, doctrine, discipline, and mobility. The Arabic daily press recorded gunfire, deaths, village cordons, house searches, and restrictions on movement. That distinction matters. These were not always independent accounts. Some Arabic articles drew on government communiqués, and others explicitly summarized Hebrew newspapers.

The resulting workflow is fairly simple. First, bound the question by time, language, archive, and non-goals. Next, build a multilingual vocabulary that includes variant spellings, places, infrastructure, and operational terms. Preserve failed searches. Enter every candidate source in a manifest with its stable link, date, information origin, verification status, and rights note. Finally, build an event crosswalk. Link two accounts only when their date, place, and at least two operational details overlap. Classify the proposed relation as confirmed, probable, theater-level, or rejected.

AI helped expand spellings, triage OCR results, translate, group candidates, and propose comparisons. The limit was deliberate. It did not establish that two records described the same event. It did not repair an uncertain name, supply missing text, or treat an Arabic newspaper as an unmediated voice of the revolt. Those decisions remained with the researcher and were checked against the article page, the scan, and the report’s information chain.

This approach has clear precedents. Collections as Data treats documentation, provenance, rights, and absence as parts of the scholarly object. The Environmental Scan measures the partiality of large historical newspaper datasets. Impresso develops multilingual access to historical media. Computational grounded theory alternates computational discovery with close interpretation and confirmation. Human-in-the-loop methods for qualitative coding and systematic reviews retain researcher control while using models to scale classification and screening.

The additional contribution is modest. It joins those practices around a particular unit of analysis: the contested event. The crosswalk does not merge sources into one authoritative story. It preserves their distance, their dependence on other institutions, and the uncertainty of the match. Negative searches and unresolved leads remain part of the record.

For historical and security research, this may be one of the more useful roles for artificial intelligence. The model does not tell the past. It functions as a research assistant that broadens the field of view while leaving a trail that another scholar can inspect. The value lies not only in what the search finds, but in whether we can explain how it was found, why two sources were connected, where uncertainty remains, and when the inquiry should stop or reopen.
